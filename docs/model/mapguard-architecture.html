01 — Problem

Anyone can edit the map. Nobody checks first.

OpenStreetMap accepts roughly 50,000 changesets per day with no pre-approval. Its data feeds Apple Maps, Meta, Amazon Logistics, every open routing engine, and humanitarian mapping for disaster response. A bad edit becomes a broken route.

The damage arrives in distinct forms, and each leaves a different fingerprint:

ClassExampleDetectable by
Geometry vandalismFake town drawn in open ocean; a road node dragged 400 kmshape, displacement
Semantic vandalismOffensive or gibberish text written into a name tagtext content
Destructive editsLarge fraction of a city deletedscale, operation mix
Careless newbie damageOne node dragged; a road stretches across a countrygeometry, user history
Broken imports10,000 duplicate buildingschangeset size, repetition

The current defence is largely manual: volunteers reading changesets by hand in OSMCha, assisted by rule-based flags with poor precision.

The modern pattern for abuse detection is a self-supervised foundation encoder over event sequences, fine-tuned per task, with entity embeddings as a first-class input. Nobody has applied it to OSM. That is this project.

Every F1 above is measured on a 50/50 balanced test set. Real prevalence is far below 1%. None of these numbers describe operational performance — and that gap is one of our contributions.

03 — Gaps

What the state of the art throws away

Read from Ovid's feature tables directly, not its abstract. Its inputs are: changeset counts and a bounding box; aggregate user statistics; and per-edit metadata in which the entire content of a name change is compressed to name_changed ∈ {0,1}.

  • G1No real geometryA bounding box only — no shape, no displacement, no area change, no topology. Ovid itself notes the one geometry-aware prior work is "limited to the building domain."
  • G2No tag contentWhat the vandal actually typed is never read. Their own GloVe+CNN baseline shows text carries signal they discard.
  • G3User history is aggregates, not a sequenceCounts and dates. No temporal order — despite Meta shipping user-sequence embeddings to production in 2021.
  • G4Large changesets are guttedAbove a threshold the edit vector is zeroed and the model falls back to metadata. Mass vandalism and broken imports are large changesets.
  • G5Balanced-set evaluationNegatives randomly downsampled to match positives, so reported F1 says little about a real review queue.
  • G6No temporal generalisation testTrained and tested on 2014–2019, never evaluated on later data. OSM's editor mix and tag conventions have shifted since.
Independent confirmation

The 2024 survey ML Updates for OpenStreetMap: Analysis of Research Gaps and Future Directions (arXiv 2407.03365) independently names as open gaps: pretraining on unlabelled OSM data "despite its abundance"; geometry beyond point/polygon classification; and inconsistent benchmarking practices. Our three contributions are three of the gaps a survey explicitly asked for.

04 — Approach

Learn normal for free, then fine-tune for damage

Teach a network what normal map editing looks like from millions of unlabelled edits, then fine-tune it on the 18,276 labelled changesets — and give it the signal every prior model discards.

Two stages, following the Stripe pattern. Stage 1 learns a 128-d representation of "a normal OSM edit" with no labels. Stage 2 loads those weights, adds the geometry, text, sequence and user branches, and fine-tunes.

Why fusing beats either alone: masked tag modelling on its own is a solved, unremarkable task. As pretraining it answers the field's stated blocker — Ovid's own abstract names "the lack of annotated data" as the core difficulty.

06 — Data

Three tiers, one verified shortcut

The labels are downloaded and inspected: 18,276 changesets, exactly 9,138 damaging and 9,138 clean, IDs spanning 19,739,784–79,067,468 (2014–2019).

Changeset contents come from the public real-changesets bucket on AWS Open Data — no account, no signing, minutely updated. Each element carries an old block:

{"elements": [{ "id": "494555506", "version": "6", "changeset": "147186571", "uid": "15324596", "user": "dmfr", "old": { "version": "5", "action": "modify", "type": "way", "tags": {"landuse": "landfill", "name": "Tratolixo — Ecoparque"}, "nodes": [{"ref": "4863084239", "lat": "38.9390456", "lon": "-9.2914563"}, …] } }]}

Before and after tags, plus before/after node coordinates — the geometry branch handed to us directly, with no history file and no geometry reconstruction. But only inside the bucket's window, and Gate 0 established that the window does not reach the labels.

Gate 0 — result: failed

Probed 140 labelled changeset IDs, stratified across the full 19.7M–79.1M range and both classes. 0 hits. All 404. Uniform across every stratum — not a partial cutoff, but zero coverage. real-changesets does not hold 2014–2019 changesets at all.

Caught on day one rather than in week six. Cost of the failure: ~20 person-hours, exactly as budgeted in the fallback.

Two follow-up gates established the replacement:

QuestionResult
Does /api/0.6/changeset/<id>/download work on old changesets?✅ 3/3 · HTTP 200 · ~0.2 s each · full tag diffs, operations, versions, inline lat/lon for nodes
Does /api/0.6/<type>/<id>/<version-1> return the previous state?✅ 2/2 · but ways return <nd> refs without coordinates
Where does the bucket actually cover?~147M – 164M · Feb 2024 – Mar 2025 · sharp cutoff between 164M and 165M
Current maximum changeset ID188,495,159 (2026-09-03)

Two consequences. The registry's "minutely updated" claim is stale — the bucket stopped being fed around early 2025. And historical way geometry is expensive through the API, since a previous version yields node references rather than coordinates.

What this forces — and why it's better than the plan it replaced

The corpus splits in two, each half doing the job it is actually good at:

Corpus A — Ovid-eraCorpus B — Modern
Period2014–2019Feb 2024 – Mar 2025
SourceOSM API, per changesetreal-changesets S3, bulk

This is stronger than the single-corpus plan it replaces. The temporal contribution G6 sharpens from "test on later data" into a genuine cross-corpus generalisation study, and building a modern labelled benchmark becomes a contribution in its own right — precisely the "inconsistent benchmarking practices" gap the 2024 survey named.

One open item

Corpus B needs labels. Two independent routes, either sufficient: the OSMCha harmful flag (human-reviewed, cleanest — needs a free token) and revert mining from the 8.1 GB changeset dump (no account, runs on Colab). Do both — OSMCha for precision, revert mining for volume.

Environment — local Python is 3.14

TensorFlow does not support Python 3.14. Local work needs a 3.12 virtual environment. Nothing on the critical path depends on it — local Python is only for baselines and figures, and Colab and Kaggle both ship supported versions.

Tooling

DuckDB is the key choice for an 8 GB machine — it queries Parquet larger than RAM. Paired with Polars over pandas for the same reason. Keras 3 on TensorFlow for the model, since the course labs are TF/Keras and its functional API handles multi-input cleanly; PyTorch is the alternative if the team prefers it, but decide once, in week 1. Shapely and GeoPandas for geometry features, scikit-learn and XGBoost for baselines, Weights & Biases for the ablation grid, LaTeX on Overleaf for the paper.

No paid APIs. No LLM anywhere in the pipeline — matching Wikimedia's own finding that LLM-based revert risk is too slow to serve.

R4 produces the single most convincing figure in the paper: pretrained against from-scratch across label budgets. If the pretrained curve sits above the scratch curve — especially at 10% — the whole thesis is demonstrated in one plot.

Expected results, stated in advance

RegimeExpectationConfidence
R1 balanced+2 to +6 F1 over 81.66medium — could be +1
R2 realisticF1 collapses for everyone to ~0.2–0.4; PR-AUC becomes the real metrichigh that it collapses; the number is the finding
R3 temporalmeasurable drop on 2026 datahigh
R4 label efficiencypretrained above scratch, gap widest at 10%medium-high
Neither failure mode sinks the project

If geometry adds little — plausible if most vandalism is tag-only — the finding becomes "geometry helps only the geometric subclass," supported by a per-vandalism-type breakdown nobody has published. If pretraining doesn't help, that means 18k labels is already sufficient, which contradicts the field's stated assumption including Ovid's own abstract. Both are publishable. That is deliberate.
